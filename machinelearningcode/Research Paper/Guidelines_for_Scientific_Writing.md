# Guidelines for Scientific Writing

Scientific writing is a critical skill for effectively communicating research findings, hypotheses, and analyses. These guidelines provide a structured approach to writing scientific papers, reports, and articles.

---

## 1. Content Flow and Organization

A well-structured scientific document should have a smooth, logical flow that guides the reader through the content.

### Key Guidelines

- **Begin with an outline:** Before writing, create an outline that maps the main sections and key points. This helps maintain a clear structure.
- **Use transitional phrases:** Use words and phrases such as *furthermore*, *additionally*, *in contrast*, and *as a result* to connect ideas smoothly.
- **Organize information logically:** Present information in a logical sequence, moving from general background to specific research. Each paragraph should build upon the previous one and lead naturally into the next.

---

# 2. Combining Various Content Types

Scientific documents often use different content types to communicate information effectively.

## 2.1 Paragraphs

- Use each paragraph to discuss a single main idea or topic.
- Keep paragraphs concise and focused, typically **3–5 sentences**.
- Begin each paragraph with a **topic sentence** that introduces the main point.

## 2.2 Lists

- Use bulleted or numbered lists to present related items or steps.
- Keep list items grammatically and structurally parallel.
- Introduce a list with a complete sentence followed by a colon.

## 2.3 Figures and Charts

- Use figures and charts to visually represent data, concepts, or processes.
- Refer to every figure or chart within the text.
- Provide a brief description of what the figure or chart demonstrates.
- Number figures sequentially, for example:
  - Figure 1
  - Figure 2
  - Figure 3
- Provide descriptive captions for all figures.

## 2.4 Pseudocode and Flowcharts

Use pseudocode and flowcharts to represent algorithms, procedures, or processes.

### Algorithms

- Refer to each algorithm within the text.
- Briefly describe its purpose.
- Number algorithms sequentially:
  - Algorithm 1
  - Algorithm 2
- Provide descriptive captions.

### Flowcharts

If a flowchart represents an algorithm, it should be treated and numbered as an **algorithm**.

If a flowchart does **not** represent an algorithm, number it as a **figure** and provide a descriptive caption.

## 2.5 Tables

- Use tables to present large amounts of data or complex information in an organized manner.
- Refer to each table within the text.
- Explain the purpose of the table or highlight important findings.
- Number tables sequentially:
  - Table 1
  - Table 2
  - Table 3
- Give each table a descriptive title.

## 2.6 Equations

- Present mathematical and statistical equations using appropriate notation.
- Number equations sequentially, normally using parentheses:
  - (1)
  - (2)
  - (3)
- Explain the variables and symbols used in each equation.

# 3. Hierarchical Approach to Writing

A scientific document should be developed hierarchically, beginning with the overall structure and progressively refining individual sections, paragraphs, and sentences.

## 3.1 Report Structure

A scientific report usually follows this structure:

1. Title Page
2. Abstract
3. Introduction
4. Literature Review
5. Methodology
6. Results
7. Discussion
8. Conclusion
9. References
10. Appendices, if applicable

The exact structure may vary depending on the type of report and the requirements of the course, institution, or publication.

## 3.2 Chapter and Section Organization

- Divide the main body into chapters or sections according to content and purpose.
- Use descriptive headings and subheadings.
- Ensure each section flows logically from the previous section.
- Make sure every section contributes to the overall research narrative.
- Aim for **at least 3–5 paragraphs per major section** where appropriate.
- Avoid sections containing only 1–2 sentences unless the content genuinely requires brevity.
- If a section is too short, consider:
  - Expanding the discussion
  - Combining it with a related section
  - Moving the content to a more appropriate section
  - Removing it if it does not contribute to the document

## 3.3 Paragraph Structure

A strong paragraph should generally follow this structure:

```text
Topic sentence
      ↓
Supporting evidence / explanation
      ↓
Analysis or interpretation
      ↓
Concluding or transitional sentence
```

### Paragraph Guidelines

- Begin with a topic sentence introducing the main idea.
- Provide supporting evidence, examples, explanations, or citations.
- Analyse or explain the evidence rather than simply listing information.
- End with a summary or transitional statement where appropriate.
- Aim for approximately **100–200 words** when suitable for the topic.
- Avoid one-sentence paragraphs because they often lack sufficient development and can interrupt the flow of the document.

## 3.4 Word Choice

Selecting appropriate words is important for clarity and precision.

- Use discipline-specific terminology accurately and consistently.
- Avoid unnecessary jargon.
- Prefer simpler wording when it communicates the same idea effectively.
- Consider the meaning and connotations of words carefully.
- Define acronyms and abbreviations at first use.
- Use a formal and objective academic tone.
- Avoid:
  - Colloquialisms
  - Slang
  - Informal expressions
  - Unnecessary emotive language

## 3.5 Content Adequacy

Every chapter, section, and subsection should contain sufficient content to address its purpose.

Ensure that the document provides:

- Relevant background
- Clear explanations
- Appropriate examples
- Supporting evidence
- Analysis
- Appropriate citations

If a section feels underdeveloped:

1. Expand the explanation.
2. Add relevant evidence or literature.
3. Merge it with a related section.
4. Reassess whether the section is necessary.

Multiple rounds of revision should be used to improve balance, clarity, and coherence throughout the document.

# 4. Sentence Structure

Effective sentence structure improves readability and helps communicate research clearly.

## 4.1 Sentence Complexity

Aim for a balance between simple and complex sentences.

### Simple sentences

Use simple sentences for:

- Straightforward facts
- Key findings
- Important conclusions

### Complex sentences

Use complex sentences when explaining:

- Relationships between concepts
- Causes and effects
- Research arguments
- More complicated ideas

However, avoid unnecessarily long or convoluted sentences.

**Good practice:** If a sentence contains too many ideas, divide it into two or more sentences.

## 4.2 Active vs. Passive Voice

Prefer **active voice** for most scientific writing because it is generally clearer and more direct.

**Active:**

> The researchers conducted the experiment.

**Passive:**

> The experiment was conducted by the researchers.

Passive voice can still be appropriate when:

- The action is more important than the actor.
- The actor is unknown.
- The focus should remain on the procedure or result.

**Example:**

> The samples were analysed using gas chromatography.

## 4.3 Verb Tense

Use verb tense consistently and according to the purpose of the statement.

### Present tense

Use present tense for:

- Generally accepted facts
- Established knowledge
- Ongoing conditions
- Conclusions that remain applicable

**Example:**

> The Earth revolves around the Sun.

### Past tense

Use past tense for:

- Specific research procedures
- Experiments that were conducted
- Observations
- Results from a completed study

**Example:**

> The participants completed a survey questionnaire.

## 4.4 Parallelism

Maintain parallel grammatical structure when presenting lists or a series of related ideas.

**Example:**

> The study aims to investigate the effects of temperature, pressure, and humidity on the reaction rate.

Avoid mixing grammatical structures unnecessarily within the same list.

# 5. Citations and Referencing

Proper citation and referencing are essential for acknowledging sources and avoiding plagiarism.

## Guidelines

- Cite sources within the text using the citation style required by your institution or publication.
- Common styles include:
  - APA
  - IEEE
  - ACM
- Include a complete reference list at the end of the document.
- Ensure every in-text citation corresponds to an entry in the reference list.
- Ensure every reference-list entry is cited appropriately in the text where required.
- Use one citation style consistently throughout the document.
- Cite original research when making important technical or factual claims.
- Avoid relying excessively on secondary sources when the original source is available.

# 7. Scientific Writing Checklist

Before submitting a scientific report, check the following.

## Structure

- [ ] Does the report have a clear overall structure?
- [ ] Do sections follow a logical order?
- [ ] Are headings and subheadings descriptive?
- [ ] Does each section contribute to the research narrative?

## Paragraphs

- [ ] Does each paragraph have one main idea?
- [ ] Does each paragraph begin with a clear topic sentence?
- [ ] Is sufficient evidence or explanation provided?
- [ ] Are paragraphs adequately developed?
- [ ] Are unnecessary one-sentence paragraphs avoided?

## Figures and Tables

- [ ] Is every figure referred to in the text?
- [ ] Does every figure have a descriptive caption?
- [ ] Are figures numbered sequentially?
- [ ] Is every table referred to in the text?
- [ ] Does every table have a descriptive title?
- [ ] Are tables and figures used to support the argument rather than simply decorate the report?

## Academic Language

- [ ] Is the writing formal and objective?
- [ ] Is terminology used consistently?
- [ ] Are acronyms defined at first use?
- [ ] Is unnecessary jargon avoided?
- [ ] Are sentences clear and appropriately structured?

## Citations

- [ ] Are important claims supported by appropriate sources?
- [ ] Are citations formatted consistently?
- [ ] Does every in-text citation have a corresponding reference?
- [ ] Is the required citation style being followed?

## Final Review

- [ ] Check grammar and spelling.
- [ ] Check sentence clarity.
- [ ] Check transitions between paragraphs.
- [ ] Check transitions between sections.
- [ ] Check that claims are supported by evidence.
- [ ] Check that the conclusion directly addresses the research aim/questions.
- [ ] Proofread the complete document more than once.

---

# 8. Key Principle

A strong scientific report should not simply **present information**. It should develop a clear and logical argument supported by appropriate evidence.

A useful writing pattern is:

```text
Claim
  ↓
Evidence
  ↓
Explanation
  ↓
Analysis
  ↓
Link to research question
```

The reader should be able to understand:

1. **What is the problem?**
2. **Why is the problem important?**
3. **What has previous research found?**
4. **What gap remains?**
5. **What did this study do?**
6. **What did the study find?**
7. **What do the findings mean?**
8. **How does the study contribute to the field?**
